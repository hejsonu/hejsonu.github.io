Photo Print Size Checker

Most "pixels to inches" calculators only divide by 300. They skip the part that surprises people at the print counter: an 8x10 print does not have the same shape as a camera or phone photo, so part of the picture is cut off — and the resolution is measured on what is left.

How to use it

  1. Find the photo's size in pixels. On a phone, open the photo's details (the "i" button); on a computer, check the file's properties. A 12-megapixel phone photo is usually 4032 x 3024; a 24-megapixel camera photo is usually 6000 x 4000.
  2. Pick the print size. Portrait or landscape does not matter — the tool turns the print to match your photo.
  3. Read the result: how much of the image is cut off to fill the paper, which sides lose it, and the DPI (dots per inch) of the part that is printed.

Rough guide to DPI: 300 or more looks sharp even up close; 200–299 looks good in a frame at normal distance; 150–199 is usually fine for a large print on a wall; below 150 you may see softness. Print labs use similar guides, but viewing distance matters more than any single number. Upscaling a file adds pixels, not detail.

Worked examples

1. 24-megapixel camera photo (6000 x 4000) as an 8x10

The photo is 3:2 (1.5 times as wide as tall); 8x10 is 5:4 (1.25). To fill the paper, the width is trimmed to 4000 x 1.25 = 5000 pixels, so 1000 pixels — 16.7% of the picture — are cut from the sides. What remains prints at 4000 ÷ 8 = 500 DPI: plenty. Cropping, not resolution, is the thing to plan for here.

2. 12-megapixel phone photo (4032 x 3024) as a 4x6

Phone photos are 4:3 (1.33); a 4x6 is 3:2 (1.5) — longer. This time the top and bottom are trimmed: 4032 ÷ 1.5 = 2688 of the 3024 pixels stay, so 11.1% is cut off. The print is 672 DPI. Heads close to the top edge of a phone photo are what usually get clipped.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my 8x10 print cut off part of my photo?

Because the shapes differ. Most cameras make 3:2 photos and most phones make 4:3 photos, but an 8x10 is 5:4 — closer to a square. Filling the paper means trimming the long side. If you want nothing cut, choose a print size with the same shape (4x6, 8x12, 12x18 or 20x30 for 3:2 photos), or ask for a "fit" print with white borders.

Is 300 DPI really required?

No. 300 DPI is the safe number for prints you hold in your hand. Larger prints are looked at from further away, so 150–200 DPI is usually acceptable for posters and wall prints. The tool shows the DPI after cropping, which is the number that matters.

Does the DPI setting saved in the file matter?

Not for the print size. The "72 DPI" or "300 DPI" tag in a file is just a label; the printer uses the pixels. A 3000 x 2400 file prints the same 10x8 inches at 300 DPI whatever the tag says.
