Stacked Discount Calculator

"30% off, plus an extra 20% off at checkout" is not 50% off. Enter the original price, every percent discount, an optional dollar coupon and your sales tax to see the real price, step by step.

How to use it

  1. Enter the original (tag) price before any discount.
  2. List the percent discounts separated by commas, in the order the store applies them — usually the advertised sale first, then the "extra" checkout discount.
  3. If you also have a dollar-off coupon ("$10 off"), enter it and choose whether it is taken before or after the percent discounts. The receipt or the coupon's fine print usually tells you; if you are not sure, try both — the tool shows the difference.
  4. Enter your sales tax rate to see what you will actually pay. Tax is calculated on the discounted price.

Why stacking is less than the sum: each percent discount is taken from the price that is left after the previous one. 30% then 20% means you pay 0.70 × 0.80 = 0.56 of the price, which is 44% off, not 50%. The order of the percent discounts does not change the result; the position of a dollar coupon does. Tax rules for coupons (especially manufacturer coupons) differ between US states, so treat the tax line as an estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Does the order of percent discounts matter?

No. Multiplication works in any order, so 20% then 30% gives the same price as 30% then 20%. Only a fixed-dollar coupon changes the result depending on when it is applied.

How do I turn stacked discounts into one percentage?

Convert each discount to the share you still pay (30% off → 0.70), multiply the shares together, and subtract the result from 1. For 30% and 20%: 1 − 0.70 × 0.80 = 0.44, so 44% off. The calculator shows this as "real discount".

Is sales tax charged on the original price or the sale price?

For ordinary store discounts, tax is normally charged on the reduced price, which is what this tool assumes. Manufacturer coupons are treated differently in some states, so for those the tax line may be slightly off. Check your state's rule or your receipt if the exact cents matter.
