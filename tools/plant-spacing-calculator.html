Plant Spacing Calculator

How many plants will really fit in your bed? Most spacing calculators divide the area by the spacing, which ignores the edges. This one places every plant, keeps a gap from the border, compares a square grid with a staggered (triangular) layout, and draws the plan.

How to use it

  1. Enter the inside length and width of the bed (for a raised bed, measure inside the boards).
  2. Enter the spacing from the plant tag or seed packet — the distance from one plant's center to the next.
  3. The gap from the edge defaults to half the spacing, so plants on the border get roughly the same room as the ones inside. Set it to 0 if plants may sit right at the edge, or larger next to a path.
  4. Press Calculate. You get the count for a square grid and for the best staggered layout (rows tried in both directions), the area-only estimate for comparison, and a drawing of the layout that fits more plants.

Square grid: plants in straight rows and columns, all neighbors at the spacing distance. Staggered (triangular): every other row is shifted by half a spacing and rows sit closer (0.866 × spacing apart), so each plant is still exactly one spacing from its six neighbors. In an open field this fits about 15% more plants — but in a small bed the shifted rows lose a plant at the edge, and the gain can disappear.

Worked examples

1. 4 × 8 ft raised bed, 12-inch spacing

With a 6-inch edge gap, the plant centers fit in a 3 × 7 ft area. A square grid gives 4 rows of 8 = 32 plants. The best staggered layout (rows across the bed) also gives 32. The area-only formula would promise 32 square / 37 staggered — the 5 "extra" staggered plants do not actually fit.

2. 10 × 20 ft ground-cover bed, 12-inch spacing

Square grid: 10 rows of 20 = 200 plants. Staggered with rows along the 20 ft side: 11 rows alternating 20 and 19 plants = 215 plants, 7.5% more. (Rows the other way give 209.) The area-only formula says 231, so buying plants by that number would leave you with 16 spare.

3. 3 × 6 ft bed, 18-inch spacing

With a 9-inch edge gap: square grid 2 rows of 4 = 8 plants; best staggered layout only 7 (rows of 4 and 3). Here the simple square grid is the better choice.

Frequently asked questions

Is triangular (staggered) spacing always better?

No. It packs more plants per square foot in large areas, which is why it is popular for ground covers and hedges. In a narrow or small bed the shifted rows often lose one plant each, as examples 1 and 3 show. Use the drawing to decide; a square grid is also easier to weed between and to stake.

Why use half the spacing as the edge gap?

A plant spaced 12 inches from its neighbors spreads roughly 6 inches in every direction. Keeping it 6 inches from the border means it fills out to the edge without hanging over a path or a board. If your plants can spill over the edge, use a smaller gap.

What spacing should I use?

Follow the plant tag or seed packet; it reflects the mature width of that variety. If you see a range (for example 12–18 inches), the lower number fills in faster and the higher number gives more air flow. This calculator does not recommend spacings for particular plants, because they vary a lot by variety and climate.
