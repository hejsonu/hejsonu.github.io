Unit Price Comparison Calculator

Which package is really cheaper? Enter up to six products — one in ounces, another in pounds, a third in grams, a 12-pack of cans — and see them ranked by price per unit.

How to use it

  1. Price: the shelf price you would pay for one package (after any sale price, before tax).
  2. Size and unit: the amount printed on the package, e.g. 16 oz, 2 lb, 1 kg, 67.6 fl oz or 2 L. Pick "count" for things sold by the piece (eggs, rolls, pods).
  3. Packs: for multipacks, enter the size of one unit and the number of units (a 12-pack of 12 fl oz cans = size 12, unit fl oz, packs 12). Leave it at 1 otherwise.
  4. Press Compare. The cheapest item is listed first; every other item shows how much more you pay per unit, in percent.

Weight (oz, lb, g, kg) can only be compared with weight, and volume (fl oz, pint, quart, gallon, mL, L) only with volume. "oz" here is the weight ounce; liquids labeled "fl oz" are volume. US customary units are used (1 fl oz = 29.5735 mL, 1 oz = 28.3495 g).

Frequently asked questions

Why not just read the unit price on the shelf tag?

Shelf tags are a good start, but stores do not always use the same unit for neighboring products — one tag may say "per oz" and the next "per lb" or "per count". Tags also usually ignore a sale or a coupon you plan to use. Enter the price you will actually pay and every item is put on the same unit.

Can I compare ounces with fluid ounces?

No. An ounce (oz) measures weight and a fluid ounce (fl oz) measures volume. They only match for water-like liquids, so the calculator refuses to mix them rather than give a misleading answer. Compare products that are labeled the same way.

Is the bigger package always cheaper per unit?

No. Example 2 above shows a larger bag that costs more per ounce. Sales on small sizes, store brands and odd metric sizes regularly break the "bigger is cheaper" rule, which is exactly why it is worth doing the math.
